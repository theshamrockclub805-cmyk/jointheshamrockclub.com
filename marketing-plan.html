2. Field canvassing: the door-to-door operation

This is the part of the plan that produces revenue soonest. Social media builds the conditions for a sale; canvassing makes it. At our price points, nobody is going to find us, read the site and buy unprompted in any volume — not yet. Someone has to walk in.

What one category per school actually means for selling

Exclusivity is our best closing line and our tightest constraint. Every school has roughly two dozen categories worth selling, and each one sells exactly once. That has three consequences the team should internalise:

  • The inventory is finite and visible. Eleven schools times twenty-four sellable categories is 264 slots. That is the whole business at single-school level. Knowing the number changes how you treat a slot.
  • Urgency is real, not manufactured. “One plumber per school, and Rio Mesa is open” is true. Say it plainly and never bluff about a category being nearly gone.
  • Competitors sell each other. The fastest close in this model is telling a business their competitor is looking at the same slot — but only when it is true. If it is not, do not say it.

What actually happens at a door

The team should go in with honest expectations, because the first day of canvassing is demoralising if you expect it to work like a warm lead. Typical numbers for cold business-to-business door knocking:

StepOut of 100 doorsWhat it means
Doors attempted100A dense commercial strip, 3–4 hours of walking
Decision-maker present and free25–35The owner is out, busy, or it is a chain with no local authority
Real conversation (2+ minutes)12–20This is the number that matters, not doors
Genuinely interested, takes the sheet5–10Log them. This is the pipeline
Signs, eventually1–3Usually after a follow-up, rarely on the day
Set this expectation out loud. One or two sales per hundred doors is normal and good. A volunteer who expects five and gets one concludes the product is broken and stops going out. The same volunteer told to expect one or two treats the same day as a win. Manage the expectation and you keep the people.

ActivityTimeNotes

A realistic half-day outing for two people: around 100–120 doors, 20–30 real conversations, 8–15 leads logged, and one to three sponsorships over the following fortnight.

The 30-second door script

Same every time, so anyone can run it and so the team can tell what is and is not working.

  1. Who we are, in one line. “I’m with The Shamrock Club — we’re the nonprofit that connects local businesses with the high schools here in [town].”
  2. The exclusivity hook. “We only take one [plumber / salon / restaurant] per school, and the slot at [school] is open right now.”
  3. What they get, concretely. “Your banner at the games, your name on the site and in the newsletters, all year. It starts at $497.”
  4. The ask. “Can I leave this with you and follow up Thursday?” — hand over the one-pager, write their name on it, log it.

Lead with exclusivity, not with the cause. Every business in town is asked to donate to something; almost none are offered the only plumbing slot at the school their customers’ kids attend. The cause is why they feel good about it afterwards. Exclusivity is why they say yes.

How to choose where to canvass

  • Work outward from a school, not across a city. Businesses within a mile or two of a campus serve those families already. The pitch lands harder and the banner means more.
  • Independents over chains. A franchise manager cannot sign a $997 commitment. Skip them and save the time.
  • Trades beat storefronts for the higher tiers. A roofer or an HVAC company has margin and marketing budget; a small cafe often does not. Lead Bronze at storefronts, Silver and Gold at trades.
  • One street or centre at a time. It doubles as the Neighbourhood Takeover Day content in the playbook (segment 24) — one outing produces both sales and a week of posts.

4. The Oxnard USA rewards loop

This is the part of the story that makes us different from a banner sponsorship, and right now it is the biggest hole in the plan. The three-way win only works if the third party — the shopper — actually gets something, and nobody outside the room can currently say what that is.

The loop, stated plainly

WhoWhat they put inWhat they get back
BusinessSponsorship feeExclusive category at a school, year-round visibility, and new customers sent by the rewards program
SchoolAccess and goodwillFunding and equipment, plus businesses invested in the campus
ShopperShops at a sponsor instead of a chain[to be defined] via Oxnard USA — and the school benefits from a purchase they were making anyway

That last row is the whole pitch. “Spend what you were already spending, at a place you already pass, and a school gets something out of it” is a genuinely strong consumer proposition — but only once the reward is concrete.

Decision 1 blocks the most content. Pillar 5, segments 20, 21, 13 and 23, and the consumer-facing CTA all need this answered. Four questions get us unblocked: what does the shopper receive (discount, points, entry to a draw, something else), how do they claim it (app, code, decal in the window), who pays for it (the business, us, Oxnard USA), and how does the school see a benefit from a transaction. Until those four have answers, we should not promise a rewards program in any post.

Once defined, the strongest asset is not an explainer — it is Redeem With Me (segment 21): one real person, start to finish, thirty seconds. People believe a demonstration far more readily than a diagram.

These four questions are on their own sheet. Each one is written out with the realistic options and what each costs us, plus a line to write the answer on — so it can go into the Oxnard USA conversation, or be settled internally first, without carrying this whole document along. Print /rewards-questions, one page.

6. Media production: getting footage worth using

The real problem is not the cost of a videographer. It is that most of the moments worth filming happen once. A sign-on handshake, a principal’s reaction to new equipment, a student seeing something the money bought — you cannot re-stage those, and if they are shot badly they are simply gone. Paying too little for the shoot does not save money; it converts an unrepeatable moment into nothing.

The answer is not “hire a professional for everything”, which we cannot afford and do not need. It is to sort moments into two piles and spend differently on each.

Which moments get what

TierWhat it coversWho shoots itHow often
Phone is fineField stories, behind the scenes, pop-ins, quick reactions, most Track AWhoever is there, on a recent phone, shot vertical with the sun behind themDaily
Worth a professionalSign-on days, the money-went-here reveal, owner and principal interviews, anything that will be used for monthsHired videographer, half-day, batched2–3 half-days a month
Batch the professional days. A videographer costs roughly the same for one stop or four. Line up three or four sign-ons and school visits on a single afternoon and the per-asset cost drops by three quarters. This single change is worth more than negotiating anyone’s rate.

Where the quality is actually being lost

Three things cost us more than the camera does, and two are free to fix:

  • Audio. Interviews shot in a loud shop with the phone two feet away are unusable no matter who edits them. A $30 lapel mic fixes almost all of it. This is the single highest-return purchase on the list.
  • Nobody asking the questions. A great camera pointed at someone with nothing to say produces nothing. The playbook’s four standard interview prompts fix this, and they are already written.
  • Turnaround. A sign-on reel delivered three weeks later has missed its moment. Agree a turnaround — 48 hours is reasonable — and treat a missed one as a real problem.

Option B is roughly the revenue from two Silver sponsorships a year, which is a useful way to frame it for the board: the marketing spend pays for itself at two sponsors and everything above that is funding for schools.

Total: roughly 26–38 hours a week. Split sensibly that is one paid part-time person at 10–15 hours covering editing and the feed, a lead at 3–5 hours, and 12–18 volunteer hours on canvassing and capture. It does not need a full-time hire, and it will not run on goodwill alone either — the editing and the posting are the two that fail first when nobody is paid.

What to measure, and nothing else for now

Four numbers, reviewed monthly. Resist adding more until these are reliably tracked.

  • Slots sold, out of 264. The only number that matters
  • Doors knocked and conversations had — tells you whether a weak month was effort or message
  • Sponsors renewed, once we have any up for renewal. Year two is where the margin is
  • Leads from the website — tells you whether social is doing anything commercial at all
Follower count is not on this list deliberately. It is the number people reach for and the one least connected to revenue. A hundred followers who own businesses near our schools are worth more than five thousand who do not.
